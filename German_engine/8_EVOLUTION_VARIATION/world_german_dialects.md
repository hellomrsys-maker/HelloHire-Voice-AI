# German Engine — Layer 8: Evolution, Variation & Dialectology

## 1. Historical Evolution of the German Language

### 1.1 The Second Germanic Consonant Shift (Zweite Lautverschiebung)
Around 500–800 CE, the High German dialects (Central and Upper German) separated from the Low Germanic group (Old English, Old Saxon, Old Dutch) through systematic stop-to-fricative/affricate shifts:
- Proto-Germanic */p/ -> /pf/ or /ff/*: *apple -> Apfel*, *open -> offen*.
- Proto-Germanic */t/ -> /ts/ or /ss/*: *two -> zwei*, *water -> Wasser*.
- Proto-Germanic */k/ -> /kx/ or /x/*: *make -> machen*, *book -> Buch*.
- Proto-Germanic */d/ -> /t/*: *day -> Tag*, *door -> Tür*.

The geographic boundary of this shift forms the famous **Benrath Line** (the *maken/machen* line), which divides Low German (*Niederdeutsch*) to the north from High German (*Hochdeutsch*) to the south.

### 1.2 Periods of the Language
1. **Old High German (Althochdeutsch, c. 750–1050)**: Full end vowels in noun inflections (*taga, tagum*), Otfrid von Weißenburg, Hildebrandslied.
2. **Middle High German (Mittelhochdeutsch, c. 1050–1350)**: Weakening of unstressed vowels to schwa [ə] (*tage, tagen*), courtly epic literature (*Nibelungenlied, Walther von der Vogelweide*).
3. **Early New High German (Frühneuhochdeutsch, c. 1350–1650)**: Diphthongization (*mîn -> mein, hûs -> Haus*) and monophthongization (*liep -> lieb*). Martin Luther's translation of the Bible (1522–1534) synthesized East Central and South German administrative vernaculars into the basis of the modern written standard.
4. **New High German (Neuhochdeutsch, 1650–present)**: Standardization of grammar (Gottsched, Adelung), Grimm Brothers' dictionary, and the orthographic conferences of 1876, 1901, and 1996/2006.

## 2. Pluricentric Standard German (Plurizentrische Sprache)

Standard German is officially recognized as a pluricentric language with three codetermined national standard varieties:

### 2.1 Bundesdeutsches Hochdeutsch (Germany)
- Standard pronunciation codified in Siebs / Duden Aussprachewörterbuch.
- Uses *ß* after long vowels and diphthongs (*Fuß, reißen*).
- Lexical norms: *Januar, Abitur, Krankenhaus, Quark, Tomate, Treppe, Brötchen*.

### 2.2 Österreichisches Deutsch (Austria)
- Codified in the *Österreichisches Wörterbuch (ÖWB)*.
- Distinctive legal/administrative and culinary vocabulary (Austriazismen):
  - *Jänner* (Januar), *Feber* (Februar), *Matura* (Abitur), *Spital* (Krankenhaus).
  - *Topfen* (Quark), *Paradeiser* (Tomate), *Erdapfel* (Kartoffel), *Semmel* (Brötchen).
  - *Kasten* (Schrank), *Sessel* (Stuhl), *Stiege* (Treppe).
- Auxiliary verb selection: *liegen, sitzen, stehen* form the perfect with *sein* (*ich bin gestanden/gesessen*).
- Pronunciation: No Knacklaut [ʔ] preceding unstressed vowels; distinct melodic intonation.

### 2.3 Schweizer Hochdeutsch (Switzerland & Liechtenstein)
- **Total elimination of *ß***: Replaced universally by *ss* in all official, literary, and educational publications (*Strasse, gross, weiss, schliessen*).
- Distinctive Helvetismen:
  - *Velo* (Fahrrad), *parkieren* (parken), *grillieren* (grillen), *Postcheck* (Postscheck).
  - *Spital* (Krankenhaus), *Estrich* (Dachboden), *Natel* (Mobiltelefon).
- Diglossia (*Mediale Diglossie*): Swiss German dialects (*Schwyzertütsch*) are spoken across all social strata for oral communication, while Swiss Standard German is used for writing and formal broadcast news.
